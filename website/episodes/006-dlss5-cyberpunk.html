EPISODE 006 / AI & GAMING

A different look.
Worth the frames?

Episode 006 · 90-second episode · Published

90 seconds · Captions included · Barry’s capture of an unofficial community mod

Watch on YouTube ↗ · Watch on Instagram ↗

DLSS 5 promises more photographic lighting and materials. In Cyberpunk 2077, community mods let players explore that look—and decide whether the changes are worth the cost. Barry’s own RTX 5080 capture provides our example, with an important distinction: this is an unofficial community mod, not an official Cyberpunk integration.

What does DLSS 5 do?

NVIDIA describes a neural-rendering stage that uses a game’s rendered frame and motion data to enhance lighting and material detail. Its output is one frame for each input frame. This stage changes the appearance of an image; it does not itself create extra frames. [1] [2]

NVIDIA also describes controls that let developers tune the effect and mask parts of the scene. Preserving character identity and artistic intent is the stated goal. How a particular community mod looks should not be treated as the result every official implementation will deliver. [2]

Why did the reveal divide people?

The March 2026 reveal prompted both enthusiasm and criticism. In PC Gamer’s own team discussion, some writers saw promise in the lighting, while others objected to altered faces and a loss of the original visual style. Those reactions illustrate the disagreement; they do not measure what all players think. [3]

More detail can be appealing without making every change an improvement. A face can look more photographic while feeling less like the character an artist designed.

Our look at Night City

The episode uses Barry’s own gameplay capture. It starts with the game’s Rogue Amendiares, switching DLSS 5 on and then off, before looking at Adam Smasher, Victor and Misty with the effect enabled. Our presenter, also called Rogue, guides the comparison.

The approved narration describes richer skin detail, shadows and light, alongside changes to familiar faces. That is our assessment of the selected footage, not a universal quality verdict. Watch the moving comparison as well as the still detail: one attractive frame cannot establish how consistently an effect behaves during play.

These are displayed frame rates, including generated frames. Turning the effect on corresponds to about 27% fewer displayed frames per second in this observation.

One system, one scene—not a controlled benchmark. These figures do not establish base rendering speed, input latency or a typical performance penalty for other settings, mods or games. We have not inferred base FPS by dividing the counters by four.

Would you trade frames for that look?

That is the question this episode leaves open. A community implementation offers a way to explore the effect, while official integrations may be tuned differently. Community developer notes also document continuing compatibility work and unresolved issues; that does not guarantee a particular performance improvement. [4]

The useful comparison is the one you can see and play: the appearance you prefer, the style you want to preserve, and the responsiveness you are willing to trade.
